## Parked Lesson GLO-B script, cues 22-62, verbatim from master

These cues are not storyboarded in GLO-A. They are parked here verbatim so the next glossary pass starts from the exact source text with no wording drift.

22. "You already know from your onboarding why the people we call matter and what kind of situations they're dealing with. These next terms are important also. We're putting specific names to the situations you're going to hear about on calls so that when a seller says something, you recognize it immediately and know how to respond. Use this video as a reference, not a one-time watch."

23. "A motivated seller is someone who has a reason to sell quickly, often at a price below market value. Think of it this way. If your TV breaks the night before the Super Bowl, you're walking into whatever store is open and paying whatever price they have. Urgency changes the decision. Motivated sellers have urgency. On a call, they might say things like "I just need to get out from under this thing" or "I've been trying to sell this for months.""

24. "Pre-foreclosure is when a homeowner has fallen behind on their mortgage payments and the lender has started the legal process of taking the home back. Think of it like getting a final notice on a bill. You still have time to fix it, but not much. For these sellers, a cash offer can be life-changing. We can close quickly, stop the foreclosure process, and let them walk away with whatever equity they have left. On a call, they might say "I've been getting letters from the bank" or "we're a few months behind.""

25. "A Notice of Default is the formal legal document a lender files when a borrower is behind on payments. It's the official start of the foreclosure process. Think of it as the lender saying "we've been patient enough, here's it in writing." Sellers who've received a Notice of Default are highly motivated. They need to act fast. If a seller mentions receiving some kind of official notice from their lender or the court, flag that immediately for your acquisition manager."

26. "When property owners don't pay their taxes, the county puts a tax lien on the property. If taxes stay unpaid long enough, the county can actually take and sell the property. Tax delinquent owners are often in financial distress. They may own the home outright with no mortgage but simply can't afford the taxes. A cash offer gives them a way out before the government takes it from them. On a call, you might hear "I haven't been able to keep up with the taxes" or "I got something from the county about back taxes." That's a strong motivation signal."

27. "Probate is the legal process that happens after someone dies. Their estate, including any real estate they owned, has to go through a court process before it can be transferred or sold. The people dealing with inherited properties are grieving. They didn't ask for the house. They might live far away. They're dealing with lawyers and paperwork on top of their loss. A simple cash offer with a quick close is often exactly what they're looking for. On a call, you might hear "I inherited this from my mother" or "my dad passed away and left us this house.""

28. "Inherited property is any property passed to someone through death, whether through a will, probate, or simply being a surviving owner. The emotional and logistical burden is usually similar regardless of the process involved. Approach these calls with extra sensitivity. Lead with empathy before you ever talk business."

29. "An absentee owner is someone who owns a property but doesn't live there. A lot of absentee owners are landlords who bought rental properties and are now tired of managing them from a distance. Think of it like owning a vacation home you never visit anymore but still have to pay insurance, taxes, and maintenance on. At some point the headache outweighs the benefit. On a call, they might say "I have this rental property" or "I inherited it but I live out of state.""

30. "A tired landlord is someone who owns rental property and is done with it. Bad tenants, late rent, constant repairs, property management headaches. They've had enough and they want out. These are often very motivated sellers because they're not just selling a house, they're selling a problem. On a call you might hear "I've got tenants who won't pay" or "I'm just done being a landlord.""

31. "A code violation is a citation from the local government saying a property doesn't meet safety or building standards. Code violations create real financial and legal pressure for owners. Fines can accumulate quickly. The city can put liens on the property. Some cities can even condemn and demolish properties. If a seller mentions the city has been after them or they've got fines on the property, that's a strong signal of both motivation and distress."

32. "Every seller situation represents a real person with a real problem. Your job is to listen for the signals. Pre-foreclosure, tax delinquency, probate, tired landlords, code violations — these are all situations where the BMH Group can offer a real solution. When you hear these signals on a call, connect that seller with the right person at BMH as quickly as possible. You are the bridge between a seller in distress and the people who can help them. That is not a small thing. Keep that in mind every time you pick up the phone."

33. "Now we will cover terms around the transaction process. You don't need to master this before your first call. But every time you hear these terms come up in a team conversation or a seller asks a question you don't recognize, come back here. It'll make more sense each time."

34. "ARV stands for After Repair Value. It's the estimated market value of a property after it's been fully renovated. Think of it like a used car with a blown engine that might sell for $1,000 as-is, but after $5,000 in repairs could be worth $12,000. The $12,000 is the ARV equivalent. ARV is the starting point for all of our offer calculations. If a seller asks how you come up with your offer, you can honestly say our team looks at what similar homes have sold for after being renovated and works backwards from there."

35. "MAO stands for Maximum Allowable Offer. It's the most our acquisition team can pay for a property and still make the deal work. It's calculated by taking the ARV, subtracting estimated repair costs, subtracting our profit margin, and subtracting closing costs and fees. Think of buying a fixer-upper car to resell for $12,000. Repairs cost $5,000 and you need $2,000 profit, so the most you can pay is $5,000. That's your MAO. If a seller's expectations are way above our MAO, the deal won't work — and that's okay."

36. "The estimated wholesale price is the specific number our team uses as a cash offer target when evaluating leads in PropStream, our property research tool. It's typically around 70% of the property's estimated value. So if a home is estimated to be worth $100,000, our starting offer range would be around $70,000. That spread accounts for repairs, fees, and profit."

37. "Equity is the difference between what a property is worth and what the owner owes on it. If a house is worth $150,000 and the seller owes $50,000, they have $100,000 in equity. Sellers with high equity can accept a lower offer and still walk away with a meaningful amount of money. Sellers with little or no equity are harder to work with because there's no room between what they owe and what we can pay. On a call you might ask "do you have a mortgage on the property?" That question tells us a lot about whether the deal has legs."

38. "The repair estimate is the projected cost to bring a distressed property up to market condition. New roof, updated kitchen, new flooring — whatever the property needs. The higher the rehab cost, the lower our offer needs to be. Think of it like the car analogy again. If the engine, transmission, and body all need work, you're going to pay a lot less for that car than one that just needs new tires."

39. "The spread is the difference between what the BMH Group pays for a property and what an end buyer pays for it. This is our profit. For example, if we get a house under contract for $70,000 and sell the contract to an end buyer for $85,000, our spread is $15,000. That's the assignment fee we collect at closing."

40. "The Purchase and Sale Agreement, or PSA, is the legal contract that locks in the deal between the BMH Group and the seller. Once this is signed, the property is under contract. Think of it like a parking ticket for the property — nobody else can buy it while that contract is in place. You won't be signing purchase agreements yourself. But when an acquisition manager says "we got the contract signed," that means we have a live deal."

41. "The earnest money deposit is a small good faith payment we make to the seller when we sign the purchase agreement. Think of it like putting a deposit down to hold a venue. You're not paying for the whole event yet, but you're putting skin in the game to show you intend to follow through. The EMD is held by the title company, not the seller, until closing."

42. "The title company is a neutral third party that manages the closing process. They verify that the property has clear title, prepare closing documents, hold the earnest money, and disburse funds at the end. Think of the title company like an escrow referee — they make sure everyone does what they said they'd do and that the deal closes cleanly."

43. "Clear title means the property is free of any legal claims, unpaid debts, or disputes that would prevent it from being sold. If a property has liens, unresolved probate issues, or ownership disputes, the title is not clear and the deal can't close until those are resolved. This is one of the key things the title company investigates during the transaction."

44. "A lien is a legal claim against a property for an unpaid debt. Mortgage liens, tax liens, contractor liens, and judgment liens are all common examples. Think of a lien like a hold on your bank account — the money is there but you can't access it until the issue is resolved. A property with a lien can't be sold cleanly until that lien is paid off or settled. If a seller mentions they owe contractors, have back taxes, or have judgments against them, those are potential liens our team will need to account for."

45. "A contingency is a condition in the purchase agreement that must be satisfied before the deal can close. Think of it like a condition in a job offer — "we want to hire you, contingent on passing a background check." The offer is real but it's not final until the condition is met. The BMH Group's contracts typically have minimal contingencies since we buy as-is for cash, but the due diligence period is our window to verify the deal."

46. "The due diligence period is the time after signing a contract where our team can inspect the property, verify information, and confirm they want to proceed. Think of it like a test drive. The deal is agreed upon but we have a window to confirm everything checks out before we're fully committed."

47. "The assignment fee is the fee the BMH Group collects when we assign our purchase contract to an end buyer. It's our profit from the wholesale deal. If a seller ever asks "how do you make money?" the honest answer is "we connect properties with investors and earn a fee for putting the deal together.""

48. "The pipeline is the full collection of active leads and deals at various stages of the process, from first contact all the way through closing. We manage the pipeline in Sandra, our CRM. Think of it like a sales funnel — lots of leads go in at the top and only a percentage make it all the way to a closed deal. The first job in any conversation is to help fill the top of that funnel."

49. "ARV is what the house is worth after repairs. MAO is the most we can pay and still make the deal work. Equity tells us how much room there is to negotiate. Once a deal is agreed on, we sign a purchase agreement, put up earnest money, and the title company manages the close. You don't need to calculate any of this yourself. But when these terms come up on a call or in a team conversation, you'll know exactly what's being talked about."

50. "The acquisition manager is the person responsible for evaluating leads, running comps, making offers, and getting purchase agreements signed. In our workflow, they're typically the point of handoff after a seller has been qualified and warmed up. Think of the acquisition manager as the closer on a sales team — the early-stage conversations focus on making contact and warming up; the acquisition manager steps in to finalize the deal. Getting qualified, motivated sellers connected with the acquisition manager when they're ready — that's the mission of the early-stage conversation."

51. "Once a property is under contract, the disposition manager takes over. Their job is to find an end buyer and sell the contract for a profit. You won't interact with the disposition team much in your day-to-day, but it helps to know they exist so you understand how a deal moves from first call to closed transaction."

52. "An end buyer is an investor who purchases properties from wholesalers like the BMH Group. They're the ones who will ultimately renovate and either resell or rent the property. End buyers are the other side of the wholesale equation. They need deals. We find deals. That's the relationship."

53. "The title officer is the professional at the title company who manages the closing process. They do the research to confirm clear title and prepare the documents for closing. You won't talk to the title officer often, but you'll hear them referenced during deal conversations."

54. "A realtor or listing agent is a licensed real estate professional representing a seller or buyer. If a seller mentions they're working with an agent, that changes our approach. It doesn't mean the deal is dead, but it does mean there are commission expectations and representation dynamics at play. Always flag this for the acquisition manager."

55. "The point of contact is the specific person at a given lead who you should be reaching. In Sandra, you'll see this as the primary contact on a lead record. Not every property has one obvious owner. Sometimes there are multiple heirs, multiple partners, or a property manager. The POC is whoever our best line of communication is."

56. "Sandra is the brain of our operation. Every lead, every contact, every note, every follow-up task lives in Sandra. If it's not in Sandra, it didn't happen. That's the standard. After every call, you need to update the lead record with what happened, what was said, and what the next step is. Think of Sandra like a shared notebook the whole team reads. If you take a note and don't write it down, nobody else knows what you learned."

57. "Every lead in Sandra has a status that tells the team where that lead is in the process. Common statuses include new lead, attempted contact, follow up, appointment set, and dead lead. Keeping statuses accurate is one of the most important things you can do. If a lead is marked wrong, the team might waste time calling someone who already said no, or worse, miss someone who is ready to talk."

58. "A follow-up is a scheduled attempt to re-contact a lead who wasn't reached or wasn't ready the first time. A drip sequence is a series of follow-ups over time, often a mix of calls, texts, and emails. Think of it like watering a plant. One watering doesn't make it grow. Consistent contact over time is what turns a cold lead into a motivated seller who finally picks up the phone. Most deals don't happen on the first call. Most happen on the fifth, sixth, or seventh touch. Your follow-up discipline directly impacts how many deals we close."

59. "The appointment is the goal of every call. You're not trying to make the seller an offer. You're not trying to close the deal. You are simply trying to set a time for the acquisition manager to have a deeper conversation with a motivated seller. A callback is slightly less formal. It's when you schedule a time to call the seller back, or when they agree to call you. Either way, getting a scheduled next step is a win."

60. "A dead lead is one we've determined is no longer worth pursuing. The seller isn't interested, the property already sold, or the contact information doesn't work. Dead leads get marked accordingly in Sandra so we stop spending time on them. An active lead is still in play. The key thing to understand is that dead doesn't always mean forever. Sometimes a lead that went cold six months ago becomes very motivated when something changes in their life. Sandra lets us track and revisit those."

61. "A KPI is a measurable number that tells us how you're performing. For your role, common KPIs include dials per day, contacts made, appointments set, and conversations held. KPIs are not there to stress you out. They're there to help you and the team understand what's working and where you can improve. Think of them like a scoreboard. You can't improve what you don't measure."

62. "In CRM context, disposition means the outcome you record after a call. This is different from the disposition manager role we talked about earlier. After every call you make, you need to record a disposition in Sandra. Examples include not interested, no answer, callback scheduled, appointment set, do not call, and wrong number. Accurate dispositions keep the pipeline clean and make sure the right leads get the right attention. That's the vocabulary. You don't need to memorize all of it today — come back here whenever a term comes up that doesn't click yet. Up next, we'll look at the tools where all of this actually lives."
